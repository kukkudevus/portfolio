01 · The problem

FYERS Academy, a paid capital-markets learning platform, needed a full library of short educational videos ready at launch. Stakeholders chose the founder as the face of every lesson, giving learners one consistent, trusted presenter who wouldn’t leave partway through the series.

That decision created two bottlenecks:

{ BOTTLENECK 1 }

Filming.

Each studio shoot took at least 1.5 hours for setup, preparation, and retakes. The founder couldn’t film hundreds of videos.

{ BOTTLENECK 2 }

Editing.

Stakeholders wanted animation across most of every lesson. Manually, one animation-heavy 6-minute video took an editor 2–3 days.

02 · Discovery

Before choosing any tools, I researched what format the lessons should take.

{ LENGTH }

Under 5–6 minutes.

I reviewed studies on attention and online learning, analyzed how successful education channels and course platforms structure their lessons, and looked at platform engagement data, using AI tools to speed up and cross-check the research. Short enough to hold attention, long enough to teach one concept properly.

{ ORIENTATION }

Landscape.

Portrait video is associated with quick, scroll-based viewing, while landscape suits longer, intentional watching. These lessons were meant to be studied, not scrolled past.

These two decisions set the standard for every lesson, and they defined what the production system had to deliver.

03 · Part 1 — The avatar pipeline

To remove the filming bottleneck, I explored AI avatar generation and chose HeyGen. Getting a natural result took far more than uploading footage.

AI doesn’t fix flaws in the input. It amplifies them.

So most of my work went into designing the input.

Directing the founder for AI

To capture natural expressions, I studied how animated films record performances for lifelike emotion and mouth movement. I wrote interview questions designed to draw out specific emotions and cover every vowel sound and number, while the founder spoke directly to the camera, not to the person asking the questions.

The voice took three attempts in ElevenLabs

  1. 01A 10-minute recording, looped to 30 minutes: the voice matched, but tone and pronunciation fell flat.
  2. 02About an hour of the founder’s existing interviews, stitched together to avoid taking more of his time: each was recorded in a different environment, so the cloned voice’s tone and bass shifted mid-sentence.
  3. 03A dedicated, scripted 1-hour recording in one consistent environment: this produced the result I needed.

The final voice clone connects to HeyGen through an API, so every lesson is generated in the founder’s voice.

The result: a 6-minute lesson now takes 20–30 minutes to generate, including revisions, compared with at least 1.5 hours per studio shoot, and several lessons can be generated at once. The pipeline was later adopted for product and marketing videos beyond FYERS Academy.

04 · Part 2 — The editing bottleneck

Manual editing delivered the quality, but not the timeline. The product needed to launch within a few months, so I brought AI into the editing process.

I triedWhat happenedWhat I learned
Giving Claude Code a manually edited video to generate fromIt produced visuals, but below the quality bar, and corrections were slowCode-first generation made visual corrections hard
Switching to Claude DesignCorrections became much faster, since I could point to the exact screen to fix, but the visuals still weren’t rightThe right tool, but missing something
Adding the brand manual and animation guidelinesLittle improvementGuidelines describe how things should look, not why a visual explains a line

The breakthrough: a playbook of reasoning, not rules

I took screenshots of each scene from well-edited videos, paired them with the exact script lines, and documented why the editor chose that visual: how it represents the idea being spoken. With a few more strong reference videos, this became the first version of the visual storytelling playbook. Given a new script, Claude Design could now follow the same reasoning a skilled editor would.

A playbook that learns

I added a step that analyzes every correction made to a scene. If a correction reflects reusable logic, such as a new way to represent a concept, it gets logged into the playbook. If it’s just a wording change, it doesn’t. With every lesson, the playbook gets better.

Assembly

Claude Design can’t assemble a video, so I connected Claude Code to Premiere Pro through MCP. It places the approved animations onto the avatar footage, which also makes later changes much easier.

Editors stay in control at both ends: they approve visuals before assembly and check the final cut, while every reusable correction flows back into the playbook.

05 · Key design decisions
{ DESIGN DECISION }

Reasoning over rules.

Guidelines describe how a visual should look. The playbook captures why a visual explains a line, so the AI can apply that reasoning to scripts it has never seen.

{ DESIGN DECISION }

A learning loop with a filter.

Only reusable logic goes into the playbook; one-off wording changes don’t. This keeps the playbook sharp instead of bloated.

{ DESIGN DECISION }

Design and assembly kept separate, with human review.

Claude Design creates the visuals and an editor approves them. Claude Code only assembles what was approved, never redesigning, and an editor checks the final sequence. People set the quality bar; AI does the repetitive work.

07 · Reflection

Trust instincts, but test them

Before the brand-guidelines attempt was finished, I suspected rules alone wouldn’t be enough, so I’d already started laying the playbook’s foundation. When that attempt fell short, I was ready to move.

Every failure pointed to the next fix

Almost nothing worked on the first try, in both pipelines. But each attempt failed for a specific reason, and understanding that reason is what led to the solution.

Current limitation

The workflow still switches between Claude Design and Claude Code. It works, but it adds friction, and closing that gap is what I’d like to improve next.

What’s next

As the playbook keeps learning from corrections, I expect editing time to keep dropping, possibly toward half a day per lesson.
