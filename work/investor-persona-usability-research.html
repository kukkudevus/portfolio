01 · Context

The investor-persona assessment is part of an advisory app in development at FYERS. Users answer a series of everyday money scenarios, and the assessment describes their investor type: their traits, strengths, and what to watch out for. The app’s advice would later build on that profile.

That made understanding and trust critical. If people misread the questions or didn’t recognize themselves in the results, everything built on top would rest on a weak foundation. My job was to find out whether the assessment worked for real people before it went further.

02 · Research approach

I ran face-to-face moderated sessions with FYERS employees who invest, from long-term mutual fund investors to active traders and newer investors.

During each session, I observed how long people took, where they paused on specific questions, what they asked when something was unclear, and whether they read the introduction or skipped it.

Afterward, I asked whether the result felt accurate and relatable, whether any questions or options were confusing, and whether anything felt like too much. A short survey captured clarity, engagement, realism, and how well the persona matched them. I then synthesized patterns across sessions into findings and recommendations.

03 · Round 1 findings

Everyone completed the assessment without help, and most found their persona highly accurate. But the sessions showed where the experience was getting in its own way:

  1. 01The introduction was skimmed. Almost everyone skipped or skimmed it, so anything important there was missed.
  2. 02Vague scenarios caused hesitation. People paused or asked for missing context, such as whether an expense was essential or optional, or what “smart investing” meant.
  3. 03People read headlines, not paragraphs. On the results page, they read the short highlighted one-liners and opened longer descriptions only when a one-liner was unclear. Long results lost attention partway through.
  4. 04Jargon broke understanding. Abbreviated persona names confused people, and the word “trader” felt wrong to long-term investors.
  5. 05All-positive results felt less credible. Some asked why there were no weaknesses, and one trait contradicted others.
  6. 06People wanted next steps. Several asked what to do with their result.

The questions worked well enough to finish, but the results page, the part the advisory app would build on, carried most of the friction.

The team revised the assessment with a rewritten questionnaire, a new persona framework, and a short prompt asking people to answer based on what they usually do, not what they wish they did.

05 · Round 2 + the key insight

I tested the revised version in a second round, including the product owner and one participant from round 1 who could compare both versions directly.

{ WHAT IMPROVED }

The questions flowed smoothly.

And while people still skimmed the introduction, they read the short answering prompt carefully, confirming that short, well-timed messages get read where long intros don’t.

{ WHAT DIDN’T }

Results felt more generic.

One participant said they could apply to anyone, another compared them to astrology, and the returning participant preferred the original version’s sense of recognition. A skeptical participant asked what the results were based on.

Making the results more measured made them feel less personal.

People trust a result when they recognize themselves in it, and when they understand where it came from.

06 · Outcome
  • ■Two rounds of research, 21 moderated sessions
  • ■Round 1 findings informed a revised questionnaire and persona framework
  • ■Round 2 confirmed what improved and surfaced the precision-vs.-resonance trade-off
  • ■After round 2, the project was paused as stakeholder priorities shifted. The findings are documented and ready for when it resumes.
07 · Reflection

What I’d do differently

{ NEXT TIME }

Test with real customers.

My participants were colleagues, who may know more about finance than typical users and may have been more polite in their feedback.

{ NEXT TIME }

Align on success early.

I’d agree with stakeholders upfront on what “good enough to move forward” looks like, so research findings connect directly to a decision.

What I learned

People said the questions were clear, yet paused on the same ones again and again. The pauses were the real finding.

Observing behavior and asking questions afterward tell different stories.
